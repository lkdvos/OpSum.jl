Multi-body interactions

Everything so far has been two-body. Products of operators are not limited to pairs: * between placed operators is the operator product, in the order written, so three- and four-body SU(2)-invariant interactions are written as products of the two-body ones that physics suggests — $(\vec S_1\!\cdot\!\vec S_2)(\vec S_2\!\cdot\!\vec S_3)$, $(\vec S_1\!\cdot\!\vec S_2)(\vec S_3\!\cdot\!\vec S_4)$. The fusion channels between the sites are inside the basis, so nothing has to be named.

This page shows how products behave, which independent operators exist, and what they cost in bond dimension.

using OpSum: OpSum
include(joinpath(pkgdir(OpSum), "examples", "common.jl"))

B = spin_basis(SU2Irrep)
ex = spin_ops(SU2Irrep).S_exchange
chain(N) = FiniteChain(B, N)
chain (generic function with 1 method)

Three-body terms

Two bonds that share a site multiply into a three-site operator. The shared site is an on-site product, which is resolved in the basis of that site when the term enters the OperatorSum, so the (complete) spin-½ basis always contains the answer:

H3 = opsum(chain(3), ex[1, 2] * ex[2, 3])
build("K=3 (S₁·S₂)(S₂·S₃)", H3)
(Ws = SparseArrays.SparseMatrixCSC{OpSum.SiteOperator{TensorKitSectors.SU2Irrep}, Int64}[sparse([1], [1], OpSum.SiteOperator{TensorKitSectors.SU2Irrep}[SiteOperator{TensorKitSectors.SU2Irrep}(ITO(Irrep[SU₂](1), n=1))], 1, 1), sparse([1], [1], OpSum.SiteOperator{TensorKitSectors.SU2Irrep}[SiteOperator{TensorKitSectors.SU2Irrep}(2.2500000000000107 + 0.0im*ITO(Irrep[SU₂](1), n=1) + -0.6495190528383311 + 0.0im*ITO(Irrep[SU₂](0), n=1))], 1, 1), sparse([1], [1], OpSum.SiteOperator{TensorKitSectors.SU2Irrep}[SiteOperator{TensorKitSectors.SU2Irrep}(ITO(Irrep[SU₂](1), n=1))], 1, 1)], secs = Vector{TensorKitSectors.SU2Irrep}[[1], [1], [0]], D = 1, Ddense = 3, time = 1.7543e-5)

The factors do not commute, so the order matters, and the hermitian conjugate reverses it:

H3' ≈ opsum(chain(3), ex[2, 3] * ex[1, 2])
true

Their commutator is the scalar spin chirality, $\left[\vec S_1\!\cdot\!\vec S_2, \vec S_2\!\cdot\!\vec S_3\right] = i\, \vec S_1\!\cdot\!(\vec S_2\times\vec S_3)$, a genuinely three-body operator that is anti-hermitian (so $i$ times it is the hermitian chirality). The check is against the dense operator, which is small here:

chirality = opsum(chain(3), ex[1, 2] * ex[2, 3] - ex[2, 3] * ex[1, 2])
instantiate(chirality') ≈ -instantiate(chirality)
true

Four-body terms

With four sites there are three ways to pair them up, and so three products of two scalar products:

pairings = [
    opsum(chain(4), ex[1, 2] * ex[3, 4]),
    opsum(chain(4), ex[1, 3] * ex[2, 4]),
    opsum(chain(4), ex[1, 4] * ex[2, 3]),
]
for p in pairings
    build("pairing", p)
end
pairing                      N=   4  nterms=     1  D=   1  D_dense=    3  0.0 s
pairing                      N=   4  nterms=     3  D=   3  D_dense=    9  0.0 s
pairing                      N=   4  nterms=     3  D=   3  D_dense=    9  0.0 s

Placement may be in any site order, so the interleaved $(1,3)(2,4)$ pairing is written exactly as it reads; under a non-abelian symmetry the legs are re-coupled into site order for you (which can make one written term several stored ones). Whether they are really three different four-body operators is a question about the dense operators, and the answer is in their overlap matrix:

let ops = [instantiate(p) for p in pairings]
    G = [real(dot(a, b) / (norm(a) * norm(b))) for a in ops, b in ops]
    (; overlaps = round.(G; digits = 6), rank = rank(G))
end
(overlaps = [1.0 0.333333 0.333333; 0.333333 1.0 0.333333; 0.333333 0.333333 1.0], rank = 3)

They are linearly independent but not orthogonal (any two overlap by $1/3$), and the three-site structure shows in the cost: the pairing of neighbours has one bond index everywhere, the other two need three. Three is exactly the number of independent singlet couplings of four spin-1 operators, which is what $\vec S_i$ carries.

A note on ring exchange

The physical ring-exchange operator of four spins on a plaquette is a particular linear combination of these products: with the ring running $a \to b \to c \to d$, it is the two pairings of adjacent sites minus the pairing of the diagonals,

\[(\vec S_a\!\cdot\!\vec S_b)(\vec S_c\!\cdot\!\vec S_d) + (\vec S_a\!\cdot\!\vec S_d)(\vec S_b\!\cdot\!\vec S_c) - (\vec S_a\!\cdot\!\vec S_c)(\vec S_b\!\cdot\!\vec S_d).\]

No recoupling coefficients are needed: * and placement do it. The cost of a ring exchange is that of its most expensive pairing, which is what the ladder below measures.

Four fermions: the same, and the sign is free

The same idea holds for any symmetry, and the sign of a fermionic product is part of it. A four-fermion term is a product of two hops, $c^\dagger_1 c_2\, c^\dagger_3 c_4$:

F = fermion_ops()
ring4 = F.f_plus_f_min[1, 2] * F.f_plus_f_min[3, 4]
length(ring4)
1

Each hop is bosonic, so two hops commute, even when their sites interleave and the legs have to pass each other. * and placement carry the braiding sign of every leg that passes another, which is the whole Jordan–Wigner bookkeeping, and the check that they got it right is that the order of the factors does not matter:

let lat = FiniteChain(fermion_basis(), 4), A = F.f_plus_f_min[1, 3], Bhop = F.f_plus_f_min[2, 4]
    opsum(lat, A * Bhop) ≈ opsum(lat, Bhop * A)
end
true

Read back off the term, the forced channels are the running fermion parity along the chain — odd after the first $c^\dagger$, back to even after the first $c$, and so on:

using OpSum: bondcharges, tree
bondcharges(tree(only(ring4)))
4-element Vector{TensorKitSectors.FermionParity}:
 1
 0
 1
 0

A four-body model on a ladder

To see multi-body terms in a Hamiltonian rather than in isolation, add ring-exchange terms to a two-leg ladder. With column-major ordering, rung $x$ is sites $2x-1, 2x$, so the four sites of the plaquette between rungs $2k-1$ and $2k$ are the consecutive indices $4k-3, \ldots, 4k$ (rungs (4k-3, 4k-2) and (4k-1, 4k), legs (4k-3, 4k-1) and (4k-2, 4k)). The ring runs $4k-3 \to 4k-2 \to 4k \to 4k-1$, so the diagonals are (4k-3, 4k) and (4k-2, 4k-1).

function plaquette_ladder(Lx; J = 1.0, K = 0.3)
    bonds = ladder_bonds(Lx, 2)
    two_body = [J * ex[i, j] for (i, j) in bonds]
    plaquettes = [
        K * (
            ex[4k - 3, 4k - 2] * ex[4k - 1, 4k] +      # rungs
                ex[4k - 3, 4k - 1] * ex[4k - 2, 4k] -      # ... legs
                ex[4k - 3, 4k] * ex[4k - 2, 4k - 1]        # diagonals
        ) for k in 1:(div(Lx, 2))
    ]
    return opsum(chain(2Lx), two_body, plaquettes)
end

H4 = plaquette_ladder(6)
res4 = build("ladder + plaquettes", H4)
islossless(H4)
true

Compare against the pure two-body ladder to isolate what the four-body terms cost:

let Lx = 6
    two_only = opsum(chain(2Lx), ex[i, j] for (i, j) in ladder_bonds(Lx, 2))
    two = build("ladder only", two_only; quiet = true)
    println("  two-body only:        D=$(two.D)  D_dense=$(two.Ddense)")
    println("  with plaquettes:      D=$(res4.D)  D_dense=$(res4.Ddense)")
end
  two-body only:        D=4  D_dense=8
  with plaquettes:      D=7  D_dense=17

And the bond dimension still saturates with system size:

for Lx in (4, 6, 8, 12)
    r = build("plaq", plaquette_ladder(Lx); quiet = true)
    println("  Lx=$(rpad(Lx, 3)) N=$(rpad(2Lx, 3))  D=$(rpad(r.D, 3))  D_dense=$(r.Ddense)")
end
